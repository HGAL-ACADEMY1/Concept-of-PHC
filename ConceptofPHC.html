👋 Welcome to this lecture! Right now we shall dive into a concept that forms the very foundation of how we stay healthy as a society.
It is called Primary Health Care (or PHC for short). 🏥
Imagine for a moment: if you have a headache or a small cut, you shouldn't have to travel hours to a giant, expensive city hospital, right? You need help that is close by, affordable, and easy to get. That is the heart of what we are learning today.
Let's start with the official definition, but I will break it down so it makes perfect sense.
Primary Health Care is essential care based on practical, scientifically sound, and socially acceptable methods and technology. 🧪✅
It must be made **universally accessible** to individuals and families in the community through their full participation. And importantly, it must be at a cost that the community and the country can actually afford to maintain at every stage of their development.
Think of it as self-reliance and self-determination. 🤝
PHC forms an integral part of the country’s health system—it is the central function and the main focus! But it is also part of the overall social and economic development of the community.
Essentially, it is the first level of contact for individuals, the family, and the community with the national health system. It brings health care as close as possible to where people live and work 🏠 and constitutes the first element of a continuing health care process.
This definition comes from a very famous meeting: The **Alma Ata 1978 Primary Health Care Conference** (organized by WHO and UNICEF).
Okay, let’s now move on to the history behind this. 🕰️
Historical Perspective of Primary Health Care
Let's travel back in time to 1975. 📅
Delegates at the **28th World Health Assembly** meeting in Geneva looked at the world's health and realized something scary: the global situation was both unhealthy and unjust.
They saw numerous examples from all over the world that convinced them that a new approach—Primary Health Care (PHC)—could save people from suffering, pain, disability, and death.
They predicted that if the global community had enough political will and commitment, we could prevent a massive burden of unnecessary illness and death borne by millions of people. 🌍
This led to a new global revolution in health care, driven by a spirit of social justice. Because this was a worldwide problem, they needed a worldwide mobilization of personnel and resources.
Two major UN agencies—**WHO** (World Health Organization) and **UNICEF** (United Nations Children’s Fund)—immediately began coordinating the world’s effort to study and implement PHC on a global scale. 🇺🇳
Now that we understand how it started, let’s talk about how they prepared for the big launch.
Just like when you plan a big party, they held preparatory meetings in many parts of the world. They wanted to gather experiences and refine the principles of PHC.
Most of these meetings happened in what were called "developing" or poorer nations in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. However, they also held a meeting for "industrialized" (richer) nations in **New York**. 🗽
Why New York? They had to counter the belief that PHC was only for poor countries. No! It is for everyone, rich or poor.
A final preparatory meeting was held in **Halifax, Nova Scotia**. Here, non-governmental organizations (NGOs)—from huge international humanitarian groups to small religious groups—reviewed the final draft of the conference document.
Finally, in **September 1978**, delegates from 134 nations 🏳️ and many NGOs met in a place called **Alma Ata**, USSR (which is now Almaty, Kazakhstan).
It was a historic meeting! The nations of the world committed themselves to achieving "Health for All by the year 2000" through Primary Health Care.
From the very beginning, this "Health for All" era was based on **social justice and equity**.
In Alma Ata, they even updated the definition of "Health". Previously, WHO (in 1975) said health was "a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease."
But in 1978, they revised it. Health was now defined as:
"A state of enough physical, mental, social well-being to enable people to work productively and participate actively in the social and economic life of the community in which they live." 💪💼
See the difference? It added the ability to be **productive**. This challenges every nation to provide basic health so citizens can lead socially and economically productive lives.
Let’s now dive into the specific goals they set.
3.2 Objectives of Primary Health Care 🎯
1️⃣ To make health services accessible and available to everyone, wherever they live or work.
2️⃣ To tackle the health problems that cause the highest mortality (death) and morbidity (illness) at a cost the community can afford.
3️⃣ To ensure that whatever technology is used is within the ability of the community to use effectively and maintain. (Don't bring a machine no one knows how to fix! 🔧)
4️⃣ To ensure that the community is fully involved in planning, delivering, and evaluating the services. This is the spirit of self-reliance.
Next, we’ll look at the ingredients that make up PHC.
Elements of Primary Health Care 🧩
At the Alma Ata conference, they identified **eight essential elements**:
1. Education concerning prevailing health problems and methods of preventing/controlling them. 📚
2. Promotion of food supply and proper nutrition. 🍎
3. An adequate supply of safe water and basic sanitation. 🚰
4. Maternal and child health care, including family planning. 🤰
5. Immunization against major infectious diseases. 💉
6. Prevention and control of locally endemic diseases. 🦟
7. Appropriate treatment of common diseases and injuries. 🩹
8. Provision of essential drugs. 💊
At this point, it’s important to consider what PHC actually *looks* like in practice.
Characteristics of Primary Health Care
First, it is **Universally Accessible**. 🌏 This means care must be geographically, financially, and culturally within easy reach of the *whole* community. It must be organized and continuous.
Second, it must be **Socially Acceptable**. The care provided must be appropriate and satisfy the health needs of the people in a way that respects their cultural norms.
Third, it must be **Affordable**. Whether the community pays or the country pays, the cost shouldn't break the bank. 💸
Fourth, it requires **Full Participation**. Individuals, families, and communities must take responsibility for promoting their own health.
Fifth, we use **Appropriate Technology**. This means using methods, supplies, and equipment that are locally available and that people can actually use to solve health problems.
Sixth, it emphasizes **Self-Reliance and Self-Determination**.
Finally, the focus is **Broad**. We consider the community *as a whole* to be the patient, rather than just one individual. The word "Primary" means it is the central concept of the health system. It happens at a transdisciplinary level and permeates all aspects of society.
Okay, let's explore the strategy and philosophy behind all this. 🧠
PHC is a partnership. Consumers of health care (that's us!) are partners with the professionals (doctors/nurses). We work together for improved health.
The strategy encourages **self-care** and **self-management**. People are educated and empowered to use their knowledge and skills to improve health for themselves, their families, and their neighbors.
The goal? Individual, family, and community competence! 🏆
The key to a PHC program is not the government or the doctors—it is **the people**. They are the major target. You could say the strategy is "by the people, of the people, and for the people."
Government officials and health personnel exist to support technologies and facilities that are most beneficial to the population.
This requires the development of appropriate health technology that people can afford and use. This includes low-cost, good-quality essential drugs, vaccines, and equipment.
It also includes counseling to help people make healthy choices. And of course, it includes efficient facilities like health centers and hospitals. 🏥
Now, let's wrap up with the guiding principles.
The PHC approach is not just a "blueprint" for action; it contains a philosophy. Here are the **seven key principles**:
1️⃣ The right and duty of individuals and communities to become self-reliant and participate fully in their health.
2️⃣ Programs must reflect and evolve from the unique socioeconomic and political characteristics of the country. (What works in one country might need changing for another).
3️⃣ Programs must address main health problems, integrating preventive, curative, and rehabilitative services, sustained by a referral system.
4️⃣ Reliance at all levels on suitably trained health workers who function as a team and respond to the *expressed* needs of the population. 👨‍⚕️👩‍⚕️
5️⃣ Programs must be based on relevant research findings and experiences.
6️⃣ Government and health professionals have the duty to provide the public with relevant information about health. 📢
7️⃣ In addition to the health sector, PHC relates to other sectors (like agriculture, education, housing) and aspects of national development.
Finally, let's talk about the "Quality of Life". 🌟
PHC services are determined by social goals, like improving the quality of life and getting maximum health benefits for the greatest number of people.
Society benefits from healthier people because they are more likely to contribute to social and economic development. Quality of life relates to internal things (how you feel) and external things (social conditions/environment).
A high quality of life is evident when community members have the prerequisites for good health and happiness in their daily lives. 😊
